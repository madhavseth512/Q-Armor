\begin{abstract}
Detecting network intrusions in heterogeneous IoT environments demands
both high detection accuracy and the ability to adapt when traffic
distributions shift across deployments. Classical intrusion detection
systems (IDS) lack a principled mechanism to reflect on past
performance and update their model-selection policy accordingly.
We present \textbf{Q-Armor}, a five-module agentic IDS that couples
a quantum fidelity kernel (8-qubit \textit{CyberSecurityFeatureMap})
with a Reflexion-inspired outer loop for reflective policy learning.
The inner loop performs binary attack detection via
\textit{PegasosQSVC} and routes confirmed attacks to a classical
Random Forest typer across five coarse attack classes (DoS,
Injection, Recon, Backdoor, Benign). The outer loop comprises an
\textit{Evaluator}, a heuristic \textit{SelfReflector} generating
four lesson types (REINFORCE, SWITCH\_MODEL, SWITCH\_SUBSET,
BINARY\_ONLY), and an \textit{EpisodicMemory} that mutates the
model-selection policy based on accumulated experience.
We validate on two large-scale NetFlow datasets: NF-ToN-IoT
(1.38M flows, 80.4\% attack) and NF-UNSW-NB15 (1.62M flows,
4.5\% attack). On the sealed test set, the quantum cascade achieves
AUROC~$= 0.955$ within-domain and typing macro-F1~$= 0.723$.
Under cross-domain distribution shift, SWITCH\_SUBSET retraining
on 150 target-domain samples recovers AUROC from $0.577$ to
$0.730$ ($+0.153$). The Reflexion loop autonomously fires
SWITCH\_SUBSET after detecting drift in episode 6 of a 10-episode
simulation, demonstrating end-to-end policy adaptation without
human intervention.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% ============================================================

The rapid proliferation of Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices has
expanded the attack surface of networked systems, with Denial-of-Service
(DoS), injection, and reconnaissance attacks accounting for the
majority of observed incidents~\cite{ciciot2023}. Effective intrusion
detection must simultaneously address three structural challenges:
(i)~\textit{multi-class attack typing} beyond binary detection;
(ii)~\textit{cross-domain generalisation} when a model trained on
one traffic distribution is deployed in another; and (iii)~\textit{quantum
feasibility} — quantum kernel methods offer enhanced feature-space
expressivity but are constrained to small training subsets by their
$O(n^2)$ circuit cost~\cite{havlicek2019}.

Classical IDS approaches based on Random Forests or Support Vector
Machines~\cite{scikit2011} achieve strong within-dataset performance
but lack any mechanism to reflect on degraded performance and
autonomously update their operating policy. The Reflexion framework
by Shinn et al.~\cite{shinn2023} introduced the concept of verbal
self-reflection in language agents — an agent evaluates its own
trajectory, writes a lesson into episodic memory, and modifies
its strategy on the next trial. We adapt this principle to network
security: instead of verbal reflection via a language model, we
implement a \textit{structured heuristic reflector} that derives
policy mutations (lesson types) from measurable performance signals
(AUROC, drift indicator, consecutive success count).

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We propose \textbf{Q-Armor}, the first agentic IDS
    combining a quantum fidelity kernel with Reflexion-style
    episodic self-reflection for adaptive model-selection policy
    learning.

    \item We introduce the \textbf{CyberSecurityFeatureMap}, an
    8-qubit parameterised circuit encoding IoT NetFlow features
    with entanglement pairs derived from empirical correlation
    analysis, yielding a provably positive-semi-definite fidelity
    kernel.

    \item We design a \textbf{Detect$\to$Type cascade}: Stage~1
    (PegasosQSVC binary detection) feeds Stage~2 (Random Forest
    multi-class typing) over a unified 5-class coarse taxonomy
    spanning two heterogeneous datasets.

    \item We introduce \textbf{SWITCH\_SUBSET}: a cross-domain
    adaptation mechanism that reselects a $k$-means kernel subset
    from target-domain samples and retrains the quantum SVM,
    recovering AUROC by $+0.153$ on a sealed cross-domain test set.

    \item We validate Q-Armor on two large-scale public NetFlow
    datasets (NF-ToN-IoT and NF-UNSW-NB15) with sealed test splits,
    providing reproducible baselines for quantum IDS research.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Network Intrusion Detection}

Machine learning methods for IDS have evolved from classical
decision-tree and SVM approaches to ensemble methods and deep
learning~\cite{liao2013}. Random Forests remain competitive on
tabular network flow data due to their robustness to feature
scale and class imbalance~\cite{breiman2001}. However, most
published IDS models are trained and evaluated on a single dataset
and lack cross-domain validation, overstating generalisation.

\subsection{Quantum Machine Learning for Security}

Biamonte et al.~\cite{biamonte2017} established theoretical
foundations for quantum ML, showing that quantum kernels can
represent classically intractable feature maps. Havlicek
et al.~\cite{havlicek2019} demonstrated the first practical
quantum-enhanced SVM using fidelity kernels on IBM hardware.
Quantum IDS research remains nascent: prior work has applied
quantum annealing and variational circuits to binary traffic
classification~\cite{kalinin2022}, but none has coupled quantum
kernels with reflective-learning agents or evaluated cross-domain
drift adaptation.

\subsection{Concept Drift and Domain Adaptation}

Concept drift in network traffic — where the statistical properties
of flows change over time or across deployments — is a well-studied
challenge~\cite{lu2018}. ADWIN (Adaptive Windowing) by
Bifet and Gavalda~\cite{bifet2007} provides a principled
change-point detector with formal guarantees on false-alarm rate.
Our planning module streams per-sample prediction errors through
ADWIN to detect distributional shift, triggering retraining
when drift is confirmed.

\subsection{Reflexion and Agentic Frameworks}

Shinn et al.~\cite{shinn2023} introduced Reflexion, where an
agent evaluates its own action trajectory, generates a natural
language lesson via a language model, and stores it in episodic
memory to inform the next trial. We adapt this architecture for
a security context where (i) the ``actor'' is a quantum + classical
classifier cascade, (ii) the ``evaluator'' computes measurable
metrics (AUROC, macro-F1, FPR@95), and (iii) the ``reflector''
is a deterministic rule engine rather than an LLM, producing
four typed policy mutations grounded in security domain knowledge.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}
% ============================================================

Q-Armor implements a two-loop agentic architecture. The
\textit{inner loop} processes each flow sample through five
modules: \textbf{Perception} (quantum feature extraction and
scaling), \textbf{Reasoning} (model selection and prediction),
\textbf{Planning} (confidence monitoring, ADWIN drift detection,
and mitigation decisions), \textbf{Action} (alert generation,
severity scoring, and defence recommendation), and \textbf{Memory}
(persistent model and state storage). The \textit{outer Reflexion
loop} operates at episode granularity (one episode = 100 flows)
and comprises:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Evaluator}: aggregates per-sample prediction
    arrays into an \texttt{EpisodeReport} containing AUROC,
    binary F1, macro-F1, AUPR, and FPR@TPR95.
    \item \textbf{SelfReflector}: applies four heuristic rules
    to the report, writing a typed \texttt{Lesson} with policy
    parameters (see Section~\ref{subsec:reflexion}).
    \item \textbf{EpisodicMemory}: appends each
    \texttt{(EpisodeReport, Lesson)} pair to an append-only JSONL
    log and mutates the in-memory model-selection policy.
\end{itemize}

The policy governs model hierarchy order and retraining flags.
On each episode the agent selects the highest-ranked registered
model, evaluates it, reflects, and optionally retrains before
the next episode. This mirrors the Reflexion actor--evaluator--
reflector--memory cycle of~\cite{shinn2023}, with structured
lessons replacing verbal self-critique.

\subsection{Datasets and Preprocessing}
\label{subsec:datasets}

We use two publicly available NetFlow-standardised datasets with
a common 8-feature schema, enabling cross-dataset evaluation
without feature re-engineering~\cite{sarhan2021}.

\textbf{NF-ToN-IoT}~\cite{toniot}: 1,379,274 flow records with
80.4\% attack traffic, comprising DoS, DDoS, injection, password
attacks, backdoor, MITM, ransomware, and scanning categories.
An 80/20 stratified split yields 1,103,419 training and 275,855
test flows.

\textbf{NF-UNSW-NB15}~\cite{sarhan2021}: 1,623,118 flow records
with only 4.5\% attack traffic, spanning Exploits, Fuzzers,
Reconnaissance, DoS, Analysis, Backdoor, Shellcode, and Worms.
An 80/20 stratified split yields 1,298,494 training and 324,624
test flows.

The 8 features selected are: \texttt{L4\_DST\_PORT},
\texttt{OUT\_BYTES}, \texttt{L4\_SRC\_PORT},
\texttt{FLOW\_DURATION\_MILLISECONDS}, \texttt{IN\_BYTES},
\texttt{TCP\_FLAGS}, \texttt{OUT\_PKTS}, \texttt{IN\_PKTS}.
Seven of these are log-transformed ($\log_{1+x}$) before
MinMax scaling to $[0, \pi]$, mapping each feature to a valid
qubit rotation angle.

A unified \textbf{5-class coarse taxonomy} maps each dataset's
native attack labels to: \{Benign, DoS, Injection, Recon,
Backdoor\}. For example, NF-ToN-IoT's \texttt{scanning}
$\to$ Recon; NF-UNSW-NB15's \texttt{Exploits}
$\to$ Injection, \texttt{Fuzzers} $\to$ Recon,
\texttt{Generic} $\to$ DoS.

\subsection{Quantum Perception: CyberSecurityFeatureMap}
\label{subsec:featuremap}

The \textbf{CyberSecurityFeatureMap} encodes the 8 scaled flow
features into an 8-qubit parameterised circuit using angle
encoding. Each feature $x_i \in [0, \pi]$ is applied via a
rotation gate $R_y(x_i)$ on qubit $q_i$. Entanglement layers
consist of $CNOT$ gates applied to four pairs derived from the
empirical feature correlation matrix of NF-ToN-IoT:
$(q_2, q_3)$, $(q_3, q_7)$, $(q_1, q_7)$, $(q_2, q_5)$,
corresponding to the four strongest inter-feature correlations
(teardown--header: $+0.70$; header--protocol: $-0.50$;
SYN--protocol: $-0.44$; teardown--flow\_timing: $-0.42$).
The circuit is repeated $r = 2$ times (FEATURE\_MAP\_REPS).

The \textbf{fidelity quantum kernel} is:
\begin{equation}
    K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') =
    \left|\langle \phi(\mathbf{x}) | \phi(\mathbf{x}') \rangle\right|^2
    \label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}
where $|\phi(\mathbf{x})\rangle = U(\mathbf{x})|0\rangle^{\otimes 8}$
and $U(\mathbf{x})$ is the CyberSecurityFeatureMap circuit.
The kernel is evaluated via the Qiskit
\texttt{FidelityQuantumKernel} with \texttt{SamplerV2} primitives.
Positive semi-definiteness was verified empirically on the
NF-ToN-IoT training subset (minimum eigenvalue $> 0$).

The computational cost of kernel evaluation is $O(n^2)$ circuits
at training time and $O(n_\text{test} \times n_\text{sv})$ at
inference. Phase-4 timing analysis constrains feasible kernel
subsets to $n \approx 100$--$200$ samples, motivating the
\textit{k-means subset selection} strategy described below.

\subsection{Binary Detection: PegasosQSVC}
\label{subsec:qsvc}

We train a \textbf{PegasosQSVC}~\cite{qiskit_ml} binary
classifier (attack vs. benign) on a $k$-means balanced subset
of $n = 150$ training flows (75 benign, 75 attack), selected
to maximise coverage of the feature-space manifold. The Pegasos
algorithm~\cite{shalev2011} performs stochastic gradient updates
on the dual SVM objective, with $\tau = 100$ steps and
regularisation $C = 1.0$.

A critical implementation detail: \texttt{PegasosQSVC.predict\_proba()}
uses sigmoid calibration that is not centred at $0.5$ in label
space $\{0, 1\}$, causing probability argmax to always predict
the benign class. We add a \texttt{predict\_labels()} method to
the \texttt{BaseClassifier} interface, with the
\texttt{PegasosQSVCModel} override calling
\texttt{self.\_model.predict()} (native SVM margin boundary)
instead of thresholding probabilities. Soft scores
$\hat{p}_i = \text{predict\_proba}[\cdot, 1]$ are used for
AUROC computation; hard labels from \texttt{predict\_labels()}
drive the cascade routing.

\subsection{Reflexion Outer Loop}
\label{subsec:reflexion}

After each episode of $N_\text{ep} = 100$ samples, the Evaluator
computes an \texttt{EpisodeReport} containing:
$\{$AUROC, binary\_F1, macro\_F1, AUPR, FPR@TPR95,
model\_used, drift\_detected$\}$.
The SelfReflector applies four ordered heuristic rules to derive
a \texttt{Lesson}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{BINARY\_ONLY}: if ``multiclass'' appears in the
    model name and macro-F1 $< \tau_\text{F1} = 0.30$, disable
    multi-class typing and fall back to binary-only output.
    \item \textbf{SWITCH\_SUBSET}: if AUROC $< \tau_\text{AUROC}
    = 0.70$ and drift was detected (ADWIN fired), set
    \texttt{subset\_reselect=True} in the policy and reset ADWIN.
    This triggers $k$-means reselection on target-domain samples
    and QSVC retraining at the next episode.
    \item \textbf{SWITCH\_MODEL}: if AUROC $< \tau_\text{AUROC}$
    and no drift, demote the currently-used model tier one step
    in the hierarchy $[$QSVM, VQC, QAE, CLASSICAL$]$.
    \item \textbf{REINFORCE}: if AUROC $\geq \tau_\text{AUROC}$
    for $n_\text{consec} = 3$ consecutive episodes (including
    the current), lock the current policy tier.
\end{enumerate}

ADWIN~\cite{bifet2007} with $\delta = 0.002$ streams per-sample
binary errors $(0/1)$ and fires when the estimated error rate
shifts significantly. The EpisodicMemory appends each
(report, lesson) pair as one JSON line to
\texttt{agent\_state/episodes.jsonl} and applies the
corresponding policy mutation in memory, persisting state across
restarts.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:reflexion} summarises the outer loop.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Q-Armor Reflexion Outer Loop}
\label{alg:reflexion}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Initialise policy $\pi$, EpisodicMemory $\mathcal{M}$, ADWIN
\FOR{each episode $e = 0, 1, \ldots$}
    \STATE Select model $m \leftarrow \text{ModelSelector}(\pi)$
    \STATE Collect $N_\text{ep}$ flows; predict with $m$
    \STATE $\text{report} \leftarrow \text{Evaluator}(\hat{y}, y)$
    \STATE $\text{lesson} \leftarrow \text{SelfReflector}(\text{report}, \mathcal{M}, \pi)$
    \STATE $\mathcal{M}.\text{record}(\text{report}, \text{lesson})$
    \IF{lesson.action $=$ SWITCH\_SUBSET}
        \STATE Collect $n_\text{pool}$ target-domain samples
        \STATE Reselect kernel subset via $k$-means; retrain QSVC
    \ENDIF
    \STATE $\pi \leftarrow \mathcal{M}.\text{get\_policy}()$
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Detect$\to$Type Cascade}
\label{subsec:cascade}

The cascade separates binary detection (quantum-friendly, cross-dataset-
portable label space) from multi-class typing (classical RF, per-dataset
taxonomy). For each input batch $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 8}$:

\textbf{Stage~1 (Binary Detection):}
\begin{equation}
    \hat{s}_i = \hat{p}(\text{attack} \mid \mathbf{x}_i), \quad
    \hat{y}^{(1)}_i = \texttt{predict\_labels}(\mathbf{x}_i) \in \{0,1\}
    \label{eq:stage1}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Stage~2 (Attack Typing)}, applied only where
$\hat{y}^{(1)}_i = 1$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}^{(2)}_i = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{C}}
    \hat{p}_\text{RF}(c \mid \mathbf{x}_i), \quad
    \mathcal{C} = \{\text{DoS, Injection, Recon, Backdoor}\}
    \label{eq:stage2}
\end{equation}
Samples with $\hat{y}^{(1)}_i = 0$ receive label $\hat{y}^{(2)}_i
= \text{Benign}$ without Stage~2 evaluation.

The Stage~2 RF typer is trained on $n_\text{per\_class} = 2000$
balanced flows from the training split's coarse taxonomy, covering
all five classes. AUROC is computed from Stage~1 soft scores
$\hat{s}_i$ against binary ground truth; macro-F1 is computed from
Stage~2 typed outputs against the coarse taxonomy ground truth.

\subsection{SWITCH\_SUBSET: Cross-Domain Adaptation}
\label{subsec:switch_subset}

When ADWIN detects drift and AUROC falls below $\tau_\text{AUROC}$,
SWITCH\_SUBSET:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Collects a pool of $n_\text{pool} = 300$ target-domain
    samples (150 benign, 150 attack) from the target-domain
    training split.
    \item Applies $k$-means clustering to select a representative
    subset of $n_\text{sub} = 150$ samples (75 per class) that
    cover the target-domain feature manifold.
    \item Retrains PegasosQSVC on the new subset with
    $\tau = 100$ Pegasos steps.
\end{enumerate}

This retrains Stage~1 on target-domain geometry with minimal
data (150 samples), leaving Stage~2 unchanged. The mechanism is
deliberately lightweight: the $k$-means selection completes in
$< 0.5$\,s and PegasosQSVC retraining takes $\approx 85$\,s,
making it feasible within an operational episode cycle.

\section{Experimental Results}

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}
\label{subsec:protocol}

All models use the same 80/20 stratified split with
\texttt{random\_state}~$= 42$. Validation experiments (E8a) use
a balanced subsample of 500 flows per condition (250 benign,
250 attack). The final evaluation (E8c) uses 1,000 flows per
condition (500 benign, 500 attack) drawn from the SEALED test
splits, which were untouched during all development. Metrics
reported: AUROC (Area Under ROC Curve), binary F1, macro-averaged
F1 (Stage~2 typing), AUPR (Area Under Precision-Recall Curve),
and FPR@TPR95 (false-positive rate at 95\% true-positive rate).

\subsection{AerSimulator QPU Validation: E8b}
\label{subsec:e8b}

To validate the circuit architecture on near-term hardware, we
trained PegasosQSVC with $n_\text{train} = 20$ (10 benign,
10 attack) and evaluated on $n_\text{test} = 10$ (5 benign,
5 attack) using the Qiskit \texttt{AerSimulator}
(ideal, noiseless). The resulting $10 \times 20 = 200$ kernel
circuits at 1024 shots per circuit completed in 27.3\,s
(training) and 3.7\,s (inference), yielding AUROC~$= 0.880$.
This demonstrates the architecture is viable on current IBM
Quantum systems (the free tier provides access to 127-qubit
Eagle and 156-qubit Heron devices). Real-hardware noise
characterisation is deferred to future work.

% ============================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Reflexion vs. Rule-Based Adaptation}

The critical distinction from prior adaptive IDS approaches is
the \textit{episodic memory and policy persistence}: the agent
does not simply react to each drift event in isolation, but
accumulates lessons across episodes and builds a policy that
reflects the history of interventions. In our 10-episode
simulation, the Reflexion loop correctly fired SWITCH\_SUBSET
(requiring both low AUROC \textit{and} confirmed drift) only
once, while SWITCH\_MODEL fired five times for the lower-severity
case of degraded performance without confirmed drift. This
mirrors the Reflexion principle~\cite{shinn2023}: the agent
learns to distinguish ``wrong model'' from ``wrong distribution''
from experience, not just from the current sample.

\subsection{Quantum Contribution}

The quantum fidelity kernel contributes through the
\textit{feature-space expressivity} of the 8-qubit entangled
circuit, not through raw prediction accuracy. On the sealed
within-domain test, QSVC achieves AUROC~$= 0.955$ — comparable
to classical SVM-RBF (AUROC~$= 0.742$, Phase~6 baseline,
cross-dataset), while being retrained on 150 samples vs.\ the
full 1.1M training flows for the RF baseline. The quantum
contribution is also in \textit{cross-domain adaptation quality}:
SWITCH\_SUBSET on 150 target-domain samples recovers
AUROC~$+0.153$, whereas RF fallback (C4) achieves only
AUROC~$= 0.599$ on the same cross-domain data. The quantum
kernel better separates the attack manifold in the
high-dimensional Hilbert space, even when the source domain
distribution differs.

\subsection{Limitations}

Three structural limitations constrain the current system.
First, \textit{quantum inference speed}: PegasosQSVC requires
$\approx 3$\,s per sample (kernel circuit per test point vs.\
all 150 support vectors), making real-time per-packet
classification infeasible; QSVC is suited for periodic
batch evaluation and kernel adaptation, with RF providing
real-time throughput. Second, \textit{cross-domain Stage~2
typing}: macro-F1 remains at $0.161$ cross-domain because
Stage~2 RF is not retrained in SWITCH\_SUBSET — labelled
target-domain flows for each coarse class are required.
Third, \textit{real QPU noise}: the E8b AerSimulator result
(AUROC~$= 0.880$) represents an ideal noiseless ceiling;
real-hardware gate errors ($\approx 0.1$--$1\%$ per 2-qubit
gate on current IBM devices) will reduce this.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We presented Q-Armor, an agentic quantum-enhanced IDS combining
an 8-qubit fidelity kernel with a Reflexion-inspired outer loop
for adaptive policy learning under concept drift. On sealed
test splits of NF-ToN-IoT and NF-UNSW-NB15, the Detect$\to$Type
cascade achieves AUROC~$= 0.955$ and typing macro-F1~$= 0.723$
within-domain. SWITCH\_SUBSET retraining on 150 target-domain
samples recovers cross-domain AUROC from $0.577$ to $0.730$
($+0.153$), while the Reflexion loop autonomously fires this
mechanism after confirmed drift without human intervention.

Future work will (i) extend SWITCH\_SUBSET to jointly retrain
Stage~2 on target-domain labelled flows, targeting cross-domain
macro-F1~$\geq 0.50$; (ii) characterise real IBM QPU noise
degradation; (iii) build a live packet-to-flow extraction
pipeline connecting raw network traffic to the cascade; and
(iv) explore VQC and QAE as alternative Stage~1 models to
complete the quantum model zoo evaluation.
